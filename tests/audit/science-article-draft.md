## Scope and central questions

Philosophy of science examines the grounds on which scientific inquiry claims to produce knowledge. Its questions concern evidence, explanation, experimentation, models, the structure of theories, and the historical development of scientific practices. They also concern the social conditions under which evidence is collected, criticized, interpreted and accepted. These questions are connected, but they do not all ask the same thing. An account of how a scientist reaches a conclusion is not necessarily an account of what justifies that conclusion. An explanation of why a theory was accepted is not necessarily an explanation of why it should be believed.

One familiar question asks what distinguishes scientific inquiry from other ways of understanding the world. Proposed answers have emphasized observation, experimental testing, predictive success, mathematical formulation, the possibility of refutation, and the organization of inquiry into self-correcting research communities. Each criterion faces difficult cases. Some legitimate sciences investigate events that cannot be repeated experimentally, while some practices that appear to make testable predictions fail to respond responsibly to adverse evidence. Philosophers therefore debate whether demarcation requires one defining rule, several interacting features, or an assessment of particular research practices in context.

A second cluster of questions concerns the relation between evidence and theory. Observations do not arrive already labeled with the conclusions they establish. Instruments must be calibrated, data must be interpreted, and hypotheses are often tested together with background assumptions. A successful prediction may strengthen a theory without proving it. Conversely, an unexpected result may expose a problem in a hypothesis, an auxiliary assumption, a measurement procedure or the interpretation of a result. The philosophical challenge is to explain how inquiry can nevertheless make rational progress.

A third cluster concerns scientific representation. Theories and models often describe entities, processes and structures beyond direct observation. Some realists argue that the success of mature scientific theories gives us reason to believe that they describe otherwise inaccessible features of the world, at least approximately or selectively. Some antirealists accept the empirical success of science while withholding commitment to the truth of its claims about unobservable entities. The dispute is not settled by pointing out that science works: philosophers disagree about what kind of success requires explanation, what counts as the best explanation, and what beliefs success warrants.

The philosophy of science is also historically situated. Research methods, instruments, institutions and standards of explanation have changed across disciplines and periods. A philosophical account adequate for mathematical physics may need revision when applied to evolutionary biology, epidemiology, climate science or the human sciences. The point is not that the disciplines share nothing, but that their similarities and differences require investigation rather than assumption.

Finally, science is a collective human practice. Choices about research questions, measurements, classifications and acceptable risks can involve ethical, political and economic values. That observation does not by itself establish that scientific claims are merely expressions of preference. It instead raises a more precise question: which values can legitimately guide inquiry, where might they distort it, and what forms of public criticism or institutional accountability help distinguish the two? The debate over objectivity concerns both the reliability of results and the procedures by which communities identify and correct error.

## Demarcation, testing and methodological pluralism

Karl Popper's account of falsifiability remains one of the best-known proposals for distinguishing empirical science from systems that evade genuine tests. In Popper's framework, a theory earns its scientific status through exposure to possible refutation, rather than through the accumulation of observations said to verify it conclusively. The important distinction is between a hypothesis that risks conflict with conceivable evidence and one that can accommodate any possible outcome without revision. This criterion emphasizes the critical character of scientific inquiry, but it is not a simple mechanical test that can classify every discipline.

Testing rarely confronts a single isolated proposition. Predictions ordinarily depend on auxiliary assumptions, measurement procedures, initial conditions and background theories. When a prediction fails, scientists must determine which part of that larger arrangement requires revision. This difficulty is associated with Pierre Duhem and later discussions of the Duhem–Quine problem. It does not make empirical testing useless. It does mean that the logical relation between an unsuccessful prediction and the rejection of one particular theory is more complicated than the slogan “one failed test disproves the theory.”

Imre Lakatos responded to debates over falsification and scientific change by describing research programmes with relatively stable commitments and revisable auxiliary hypotheses. He distinguished programmes that lead to novel, corroborated predictions from those whose adjustments chiefly accommodate difficulties already encountered. This approach shifts attention from the fate of a theory at one moment to the development of a sequence of theories. It also raises a difficult practical question: how long may a promising programme reasonably persist through apparent setbacks before its defenders should abandon it?

Paul Feyerabend challenged the expectation that the history of successful science could be captured by one universally binding method. His arguments drew attention to episodes in which scientific innovation involved departures from accepted standards. That critique should not be flattened into the claim that evidence or argument never matters. The substantive issue is whether methodological rules are historically variable, whether they can conflict, and how scientists should judge a productive exception.

The resulting debate has no easy resolution. A rule that is too rigid may misclassify productive inquiry; a criterion that is too permissive may fail to identify practices that systematically avoid criticism. Philosophers must also distinguish the descriptive question of what successful scientists have done from the normative question of which methods investigators ought to adopt. Historical cases are relevant to both questions, but they cannot settle the second without further argument.

## Scientific realism and its alternatives

Scientific realism is not a single indivisible thesis. Its metaphysical dimension concerns whether the world investigated by science exists independently of our inquiry. Its semantic dimension concerns whether theoretical statements, including statements about unobservable entities, are understood as making claims that can be true or false. Its epistemic dimension concerns whether the achievements of science warrant belief in those claims. A philosopher may accept some of these commitments while rejecting or qualifying others.

Realists frequently appeal to the explanatory power of scientific success. If a theory predicts novel phenomena and unifies apparently unrelated observations, its success may appear less surprising if the theory has identified something genuine about the world. This family of arguments is often called the no-miracles argument. Yet the inference is contested. Scientific history contains theories that achieved significant success while incorporating claims later abandoned. This historical challenge has encouraged selective forms of realism, which seek to identify the components of past successful theories that were retained or approximately preserved through subsequent change.

Bas van Fraassen's constructive empiricism offers an influential alternative. On this view, the aim of science is empirical adequacy: theories should correctly account for observable phenomena, while accepting a theory need not require belief in the literal truth of everything it says about unobservables. Constructive empiricism should not be confused with the claim that scientists may not use theoretical entities or that scientific explanations are worthless. Its central distinction concerns what acceptance rationally commits us to believe.

Other positions redraw the dispute in different ways. Entity realists have emphasized experimental manipulation and causal intervention, while structural realists emphasize relations or structures that may survive major changes in theoretical description. These positions differ over what scientific practice entitles us to regard as real or known. Their disagreements show why the question “Is science true?” is too coarse to capture the philosophical landscape.

The realism debate intersects with underdetermination: whether available evidence can support competing, incompatible accounts of the world. Some forms of underdetermination arise in particular historical cases; more ambitious arguments concern the possibility of empirically equivalent theories. Their significance depends on the strength of the equivalence, the range of available evidence and the standards by which theories are compared. A careful article must not treat every case of competing explanations as proof that evidence can never favor one theory.
